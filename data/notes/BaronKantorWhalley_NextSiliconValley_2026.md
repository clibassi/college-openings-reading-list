## Paper Title
Can Government Support for Innovation Create the Next Silicon Valley?

## Basic Information
- Authors: E. Jason Baron, Shawn Kantor, and Alexander Whalley
- Year: 2026
- Journal: Journal of Economic Perspectives 40(4), Fall 2026, pp. 31-52
- Field: Economics of innovation; regional and urban economics
- Subfield: Knowledge hubs, place-based R&D policy, agglomeration
- Study type: Survey essay with descriptive county and metropolitan-area figures and case histories. No new causal estimation.

## Objective and Contribution (100-150 words)
Baron, Kantor, & Whalley (2026) ask what public research funding can and cannot do for a regional economy, motivated by programs such as the NSF Regional Innovation Engines under the CHIPS and Science Act of 2022 (p. 1). Their answer is that public R&D is "one input into a knowledge hub and not necessarily the binding one" (p. 1). The contribution is a conceptual frame of five considerations (research is an input, knowledge diffuses with frictions, complements are scarce, initial conditions matter, and creation differs from reallocation, pp. 2-3), combined with a review of studies exploiting exogenous R&D shocks and a closing case study of the University of California, Merced. The central claim is that local returns depend on complementary inputs such as entrepreneurs, risk capital and deep skilled labor markets.

## Methodology (200-300 words)
This is a review essay, so the sections below describe its evidence and organization.

**Data.** Descriptive figures use the 2015-2019 American Community Survey and 1980 decennial Census via IPUMS NHGIS, plus patent counts from Marco, Carley, Jackson, and Myers (2015), Petralia, Balland, and Rigby (2016) and the US Patent and Trademark Office (2024). Metropolitan samples are MSAs with at least 1 million people (p. 3); county samples have 2019 population of at least 250,000 (pp. 5-6). Patents are per thousand population.

**Design.** Three layers: (1) descriptive evidence on spatial divergence (Figures 1-3); (2) case histories of Microsoft, Shockley and biotech, paired with comparison metropolitan areas (Figures 4-6); (3) a review of quasi-experimental studies organized by the source of exogenous variation: endowment-return shocks, the 1980 Bayh-Dole Act, the Hatch Act experiment stations, college siting counterfactuals, wartime and Space Race windfalls, and UC Merced (pp. 11-19).

**Identifying assumption.** For the cited studies the essay stresses why the shock is plausibly exogenous, for example that Office of Scientific Research and Development contracts followed technical capability in 1940 rather than postwar promise, and that Space Race exposure interacts predetermined capability with a national escalation (pp. 16-17). The authors' own city comparisons carry no identifying assumption.

**Estimation.** None new. The essay reports effect sizes, elasticities and multipliers from other papers, and argues informally that hub formation may be multiplicative in its inputs (p. 2).

**Inference.** None. The correlation statistics in Figures 2 and 3 are printed as "R" and are not tests.

**Threats.** Selection of success cases, which the authors flag (p. 7); single-pair comparisons without inference (Figures 4 and 5); a treated group chosen after the fact (Figure 6); heterogeneity across the cited settings.

## Sample and Setting (150-200 words)
The essay's evidence base is US-centred and historical. Hub identification uses the top ten MSAs among those with at least 1 million people by patents per capita or bachelor's attainment, 2015-2019 (Figure 1, p. 4). The case pairs are Seattle versus Albuquerque (Microsoft, 1979), San Francisco versus the New York MSA (Shockley, 1956), and Boston, San Diego and San Francisco versus eight metropolitan areas with top-15 biochemistry departments in 1966 (Figure 6, p. 12). Cited studies span urban counties and university endowments (Kantor and Whalley 2014), the 1980 Bayh-Dole Act, land-grant experiment stations after the Hatch Act of 1887 with county farm data covering roughly 150 years (p. 15), World War II contracting, and NASA, whose spending at its mid-1960s peak exceeded 0.7 percent of GDP with more than 400,000 workers (p. 17). The closing case is UC Merced, opened in 2005, with more than $2 billion of state investment in its first 15 years (p. 19). A formal sample size, sampling frame or inclusion criteria for the reviewed literature is not reported in this version.

## Main Findings (200-300 words)
**Luck and initial conditions shape hubs.** Microsoft's founders moved it to the Seattle area in 1979, drawn back to their roots, and Shockley returned to Palo Alto in 1956 to be near his mother (pp. 7-8). The authors note these cases are selected on success (p. 7). Biotech clusters followed the presence of "star" scientists, not university prestige alone (pp. 10-11).

**Local R&D effects are positive but modest.** A 1 percent increase in university expenditures raises local labor income in other sectors by 0.08 percent, with effects persisting at least five years (p. 14). Industries more related to nearby university technology grew faster after Bayh-Dole (p. 14). Hatch Act station effects peak 20 to 30 years after opening and die out by 50 years (p. 15). Andrews (2023) finds 62 percent more patents per year in college-receiving counties, alongside a roughly 65 percent increase in population (pp. 15-16).

**Large shocks last but partly reallocate.** World War II contracts raised patenting in funded and unfunded local firms (p. 16). The Space Race raised manufacturing value added, employment and capital in exposed places with an implied fiscal multiplier of 0.3 to 0.4 and no detectable productivity gain (p. 17). Only about 10 percent of Houston's 6,000 scientists, engineers and administrators were local, and more than 125 firms opened Houston offices within five years (p. 17).

**Complements matter.** Lerner, Manley, Stein, and Williams find 15 to 25 percent of geographic variation in commercial spillovers is attributable to place-specific factors, and Kantor and Whalley (2014) find industries pooling labor with universities respond about twice as much (p. 19).

**Merced.** Effects so far are modest, mainly service-sector employment (p. 19).

## Key Figures and Tables

**Figure 2 — Initial Levels and Subsequent Growth of Educational Attainment.** Scatterplots for 1980-2019 against initial attainment (R = 0.43) and initial patenting (R = 0.35) for counties of at least 250,000 (p. 5). Shows persistence in human capital, a descriptive correlation.

**Figure 3 — Relationship between Human Capital and Innovation and Noneducation Sector Income.** Noneducation per capita income against attainment (R = 0.65) and patents per thousand (R = 0.61) (p. 6). The authors say these do not establish causality.

**Figure 4 — Microsoft Serendipity: Seattle Versus Albuquerque.** Median family income, attainment and patents over time around "Gates's move" (p. 8). A single-pair comparison with no inference, though similar pre-move patents and education are claimed.

**Figure 5 — Shockley Serendipity: San Francisco Versus New York.** Same outcomes around Shockley's 1956 move (p. 9). Incomes diverge mainly after about 1990 (p. 10).

**Figure 6 — DNA Luck: Growth of Biotech Versus Non-Biotech Hubs.** Three biotech hubs against eight comparison metros after recombinant DNA (p. 12). The top three hubs show median family income about $15,000 higher today (p. 11).

## Critical Analysis (300-400 words)
**What is causal and what is not.** The causal weight rests on cited papers whose designs the essay summarizes credibly: endowment shocks, Bayh-Dole exposure, Hatch Act timing, runner-up siting counterfactuals, OSRD and NASA capability-based exposure. The essay's own figures are descriptive. The step from Figures 4 and 5 to "Silicon Valley therefore appears to owe at least part of its emergence to Shockley's decision" (p. 10) goes beyond a two-city time series with no inference. In Figure 6 the treated group is defined by present-day biotech success (San Diego is included "because it subsequently emerged as a leading biotech cluster", p. 12), which selects on the outcome.

**The core argument is partly untested.** The claim that hub formation is multiplicative in research, capital and labor is stated conditionally (p. 2). The authors admit the causal effects of complements "remain largely unknown" (p. 18), and the supporting evidence is limited to Lerner and coauthors' 15 to 25 percent variance share and the roughly twofold labor-pooling heterogeneity in Kantor and Whalley (2014) (p. 19).

**Local versus national.** The reallocation point is well made, but evidence for it is thin: it rests mostly on the Houston inventor-migration anecdote (p. 17). Footnote 5 reports macro studies finding substantial national returns and calls the local-national gap "an open question" (pp. 17-18). Cost-effectiveness is quoted from "few papers" (p. 18).

**Merced.** The verdict of "modest" rests on one study of employment, and the authors say it is too soon to know the long-run impact (p. 19).

**Transfer to 2-year college openings.** The most usable idea is Andrews's runner-up-site counterfactual, which suggests testing whether open and near-miss sites for community colleges can be built from siting records. Its caution that patent totals partly track population growth (65 percent in the runner-up comparison) implies firm and patent outcomes should be per capita and paired with relocation measures to separate creation from reallocation. The essay's mechanism is research spillovers, which a 2-year college largely lacks, so its pessimism does not carry over automatically. Nothing here covers community colleges, firm outcomes or industry mix directly.

## Research Gaps and Future Directions
- Quantify complementarities between preexisting local ecosystems and added R&D spending, called a "frontier question" (p. 18).
- Explain why local and national returns diverge (footnote 5, pp. 17-18).
- Cost-effectiveness comparisons of R&D place-based spending with other programs (p. 18).
- Whether policies can attract or develop the complements in places like Merced (p. 19).
- Not reported in this version: how studies were selected for review and any quantitative synthesis.

## Implications and Future Research (100-150 words)
The practical message is that identical research grants can yield very different local outcomes, so place-based R&D programs should be judged against the presence of entrepreneurs, venture capital and skilled labor, and against reallocation from elsewhere. For program design, the authors imply pairing research spending with policies that build or attract complements, and weighing the opportunity cost of diverted public funds (pp. 19-20). For research, the priorities are measuring complementarities directly and separating local creation from relocation. For work on 2-year college openings, the lesson is to report per capita outcomes, to examine which complementary inputs are locally available before expecting firm or industry effects, and to use near-miss siting counterfactuals where they exist.
