## Paper Title
Extending the Reach of Research Universities: A Proposal for Productivity Growth in Lagging Communities

## Basic Information
- Authors: E. Jason Baron, Shawn Kantor, and Alexander Whalley
- Year: 2018
- Journal: The Hamilton Project (Brookings Institution), Policy Proposal 2018-11
- Field: Regional and urban economics; economics of innovation
- Subfield: Place-based policy; university knowledge spillovers; manufacturing extension
- Study type: Policy proposal built on a literature review and descriptive county and metropolitan-area figures. No new causal estimation.

## Objective and Contribution (100-150 words)
Baron, Kantor, & Whalley (2018) ask whether establishing research universities in lagging communities is a sound place-based policy. They review the evidence on how universities affect local economies and conclude, in the abstract, that "instead of establishing a new research university, lagging communities should focus on transferring productivity-enhancing knowledge to their local employers from existing research universities near their regions" (p. 4). The contribution is a concrete policy design: a regionally targeted expansion of the 1988 Manufacturing Extension Partnership (MEP), broadened beyond manufacturing, in which regional MEP centers partner with research universities, with doctoral and postdoctoral grants to translate research for local firms and a built-in randomized evaluation (pp. 15-18). The argument rests on three claims about graduates' mobility, localized spillovers and the weak record of new universities (p. 7).

## Methodology (200-300 words)
As a policy proposal, this section describes the evidence base and design, not an estimator.

**Data.** Descriptive exhibits only: 2015 County Business Patterns for industry mix by presence of a research university (Figure 1, p. 6); IPUMS NHGIS 2015 per capita income by metropolitan area (Figure 2, p. 7); American Community Survey 1980-2016 for college attainment (Figures 3 and 4, pp. 9-10); IPEDS degree counts for migration flows (Figure 5, p. 11). Causal claims are taken from cited studies.

**Design.** The proposal has four parts: a "targeted employment area" designation for locations whose employment-to-population ratio falls in the bottom 20 percent of US counties, averaged over the previous five years; Department of Commerce grants to universities (not required to be in the targeted area) and to MEP offices; a shift from one-time consulting to long-lasting center-university partnerships; and a focus on data science for smart manufacturing (pp. 15-16).

**Identifying assumption.** The authors acknowledge that universities are not randomly located and that simple correlations are not causal (p. 7). The proposal's logic requires that university tacit knowledge raises client firm productivity as manufacturing extension has, that spillovers are localized and benefit from face-to-face transfer, and that firms in lagging areas can absorb it.

**Estimation.** Not applicable to the proposal; the evidence is synthesized from Kantor and Whalley (2014), Hausman (2017), the UC Merced and Swedish synthetic-control studies, and Jarmin (1999) with Bartik (2018) on extension. For the program itself they propose conditional random assignment of firms in a small-scale rollout (p. 18).

**Inference.** Not applicable; proposed outcomes are firm-level wages and employment using restricted LEHD data matched to MEP client records (p. 18).

**Threats.** Absence of evidence on new rural universities, national efficiency losses from drawing activity to lagging places (p. 19), and the limited MEP-to-university link.

## Sample and Setting (150-200 words)
As a proposal, the "sample" is the evidence base and the target setting. Descriptive figures use counties with and without a research university in 2015 (defined as a doctoral university with highest, higher or moderate research activity in the Carnegie classification, p. 6), top research university metropolitan areas (p. 7), and counties with populations above 250,000 for attainment trends (p. 9). Figure 5 uses urban counties with at least one research university (p. 12). Cited studies cover urban counties (Kantor and Whalley 2014), the opening of UC Merced in 2005 (p. 14), three Swedish former university colleges granted research university status in 1999 (endnote 6, p. 21), and late 19th century land-grant colleges (p. 14). The extension evidence is Jarmin's match of eight centers in two states to plant data for 1987-1992 (p. 17). The proposed setting is lagging, low-employment communities served by the MEP network, which spans all 50 states and Puerto Rico (Box 1, p. 15). Baseline outcomes and impact sizes for the proposal itself are not reported in this version.

## Main Findings (200-300 words)
**Universities as graduate suppliers have limited local effect.** College-educated workers are highly mobile. In Figure 5 Santa Clara County gains more high-skilled residents than its universities produce, while Dane County received only about 32,000 net high-skilled residents against roughly 83,000 degrees awarded from 1990 to 2000; only 37 percent of sample counties gained more graduates than degrees awarded (p. 12).

**The main channel is localized knowledge spillovers.** Citing Kantor and Whalley (2014), a $1.00 increase in university spending raises noneducation labor income in the county by $0.89, persisting at least five years (p. 13). Spillovers are larger for research-intensive universities and for industries that share a labor market with universities or use knowledge more intensively (p. 13). Hausman (2017) finds post-Bayh-Dole employment and wage growth in university-related industries (p. 13).

**New universities are a weak revitalization tool.** UC Merced and the Swedish upgrades show modest effects: job creation in services but not in manufacturing or high-skilled sectors at Merced (p. 14). Liu (2015) is the contrast; the authors offer three reasons it may not apply today: saturated higher education, higher mobility and stronger agglomeration (p. 14).

**Extension services look cost-effective.** Jarmin finds client firms had 3.4 to 16 percent higher labor productivity growth over 1987-1992 (p. 17). Bartik (2018) judges extension at least five times more cost-effective than business tax incentives (p. 17).

**Figures 1 and 2 are descriptive.** Industry employment shares are "remarkably similar" in counties with and without research universities (p. 6), and several top-research metros have low per capita income (p. 7).

## Key Figures and Tables
**Figure 1 — Share of County Employment, by Selected Industries and Presence of Research University.** Bar comparison for 2015 across nine selected industries (p. 6). Used to argue that hosting a university does not by itself change a county's industry composition. It is unconditional and ignores county size.

**Figure 2 — Top Research Universities and Per Capita Income, by Metropolitan Area.** Map with per capita income bands from $8,400 to $50,200 for 2015 (p. 7). Shows that top-university metros, such as Bloomington, Lansing-East Lansing and Eugene, are not uniformly prosperous.

**Figure 3 — Levels and Growth of College Attainment, by County.** Scatterplots of 1980 attainment against 1980-90, 1980-2000 and 1980-2010 gains for counties above 250,000 population (p. 9). Initially more educated counties gained more, supporting the divergence narrative.

**Figure 4 — Noneducation Sector Income, by Share with Bachelor's Degree.** Scatter of 2015 noneducation per capita income against attainment in 2012-16 (p. 10). Presented as suggestive evidence of a positive relationship.

**Figure 5 — Local Labor Market Flows of College-Educated Adults by Number of Postsecondary Degrees Awarded, 1990-2000.** Net high-skilled inflows against degrees awarded with a 45 degree line (p. 11). The migration evidence behind the first finding.

**Box 1 — The Manufacturing Extension Partnership.** Describes the MEP, created in 1988 and administered by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (p. 15).

**Box 2 — University-Centered Public-Private Partnerships.** Georgia Tech with Georgia MEP and the AMP SoCal model led by the University of Southern California (p. 16).

## Critical Analysis (300-400 words)
**Descriptive versus causal.** Most exhibits are explicitly descriptive and the authors say so for Figures 1 and 2. The text states that the link between prosperity and human capital "is in fact causal" (p. 8), but the supporting studies are largely on private returns to schooling, an extrapolation to regional aggregates. Figure 1 compares industry shares unconditionally and uses a broad definition of research university that includes the "moderate research activity" tier (p. 6).

**Thin evidence on new universities.** The claim that establishing a university is insufficient rests on essentially two synthetic-control studies, UC Merced and the Swedish upgrade of existing university colleges, and the authors concede the literature has focused on metropolitan, not rural, areas (p. 14). Neither case is a new rural campus built where none existed. That is absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

**From extension to universities.** The chain connecting evidence to the proposal has a weak link. Jarmin's productivity gains are for manufacturing extension clients across eight centers in two states, with selection into clients not discussed here, and the 3.4 to 16 percent range is wide (p. 17). The proposal then supposes that adding university tacit knowledge raises returns further ("likely to yield significantly higher returns", p. 16), with no direct evidence. The Kantor and Whalley $0.89 figure comes from university expenditure in counties that host universities, so it says little about knowledge transfer to firms in distant lagging places.

**Evaluation design is a strength.** The proposal builds in random assignment and measurable outcomes (p. 18), though only wages and employment, not productivity.

**Transfer to 2-year college openings.** The paper's most relevant content is its closing note that community colleges and vocational training for left-behind communities could matter and are an open research question (p. 19), plus endnote 3's pointer to Currie and Moretti (2003) on opening two- and four-year colleges raising attendance (p. 21). The synthetic-control designs it cites for Merced and Sweden would transfer to single-opening studies, while its outcome list (wages, employment, firm productivity) is a better fit for firms in lagging places than patents. Its spillover evidence concerns research universities, so it is silent on firm and industry effects of community colleges.

## Research Gaps and Future Directions
- Causal evidence on opening a research university in rural or lagging places (p. 14).
- Whether training programs run through community colleges and technical schools produce broad regional development (p. 19).
- Whether extension programs can be directed by universities at distance and how productive that is (pp. 15-17).
- National versus local efficiency of place-based extension (p. 19).
- Not reported in this version: expected program size, cost, number of firms served or impact magnitudes.

## Implications and Future Research (100-150 words)
The authors recommend redirecting place-based higher education policy from building new research universities toward connecting lagging communities to existing ones through an expanded MEP, evaluated by a randomized rollout. For researchers, the value is the framing: separate the graduate-supply channel (mobile, weak locally) from the demand and spillover channel, and be explicit about which industries and complements are present. For work on 2-year colleges, it implies looking at firms' productivity and industry mix, not only graduate stocks, and at local complements. Future research should test the university-extension link directly, estimate effects of openings on firms in lagging places, and study the vocational-training channel the authors flag (p. 19).
